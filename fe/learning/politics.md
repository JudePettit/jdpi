# Introduction · The political spectrum

## Left, right and why the line is not enough
The words "left" and "right" come from the French National Assembly of 1789, where supporters of the king sat on the president's right and supporters of the Revolution on his left.

Today the spectrum usually arranges views by attitudes to **equality** and **change**:
- The **left** tends to favour greater equality, redistribution and reform of existing hierarchies.
- The **right** tends to favour tradition, order, national interest and acceptance of some inequality as natural or useful.
- The **centre** combines elements of both and prefers gradual, pragmatic change.
- The **far left and far right** reject the existing liberal-democratic order itself, in favour of revolution or authoritarian rule.

A single line hides a lot. Political scientists often add a second axis, from **libertarian** (individual freedom) to **authoritarian** (order and state power), which places, for example, free-market libertarians and social conservatives in different spots although both are "on the right". Some argue the extremes resemble each other in methods ("horseshoe theory"); others reject this as ignoring their opposite goals.

! How to use this course: Each unit presents an area of the spectrum in its own terms, then in practice, then through its critics. The final unit compares all five on the same questions.

= Supporters of the king sat on the right of the Assembly's president and revolutionaries on the left.

# Far left · Revolutionary socialism, communism and anarchism

## Thinkers of the far left
The far left seeks to **abolish capitalism and class society**, usually through revolution rather than elections. It builds on Karl Marx (see the Economics course), but split into rival traditions.

- **Vladimir Lenin** (1870–1924). In _What Is to Be Done?_ (1902) he argued workers left alone would settle for reforms, so a disciplined **vanguard party** of professional revolutionaries must lead them. In _The State and Revolution_ (1917) he called for smashing the existing state and creating a "dictatorship of the proletariat".
- **Rosa Luxemburg** (1871–1919) shared the revolutionary goal but warned that Bolshevik methods would crush democracy: "Freedom is always the freedom of the one who thinks differently." She was murdered during the failed 1919 Spartacist uprising in Berlin.
- **Leon Trotsky** (1879–1940) argued for **permanent revolution**, spreading worldwide rather than "socialism in one country". He was expelled by Stalin and assassinated in Mexico in 1940.
- **Mao Zedong** (1893–1976) adapted Marxism to a peasant society, built a revolution through rural guerrilla war and stressed continuous struggle against "class enemies".
- **Antonio Gramsci** (1891–1937) explained why revolution had not come in the West: the ruling class rules through **cultural hegemony**, shaping common sense through schools, churches and media, not only through force.
- **Anarchists** such as Pierre-Joseph Proudhon ("Property is theft", 1840), Mikhail Bakunin and Peter Kropotkin (_Mutual Aid_, 1902) wanted to abolish the state as well as capitalism, and warned that a Marxist "workers' state" would become a new tyranny.

### Shared commitments
- Capitalism is exploitative; private ownership of the means of production should end.
- Liberal democracy is seen as a "bourgeois" system that protects property.
- Internationalism: workers of all nations share interests against their rulers.

? What was Lenin's idea of the "vanguard party"?
+ A disciplined party of professional revolutionaries to lead the working class
* An elected coalition of all left-wing parties
* A party that would govern only through elections
= Lenin believed workers on their own would seek only reforms, so a centralised party must lead the revolution.

## The far left in power and its critics
The far left has held power in many states:
- **The Soviet Union** (1917–1991). Lenin's Bolsheviks won the civil war, banned other parties and created a one-party state. Under **Stalin**, forced collectivisation caused famines (including the Holodomor in Ukraine, 1932–33), and the Great Terror and Gulag camps killed or imprisoned millions. The USSR also industrialised rapidly, defeated Nazi Germany at enormous cost and became a superpower.
- **China** under Mao (from 1949). The **Great Leap Forward** (1958–62) caused a famine in which tens of millions died; the **Cultural Revolution** (1966–76) brought mass persecution. After 1978 Deng Xiaoping kept one-party rule but introduced markets.
- **Cuba** under Fidel Castro (from 1959): high literacy and universal health care, alongside one-party rule, political prisoners and economic hardship.

### What critics say
- Every attempt to build a revolutionary socialist state has produced **one-party dictatorship**, often with mass repression; critics argue this follows from the theory's own logic (the vanguard and the "dictatorship of the proletariat").
- Central planning delivered shortages and low productivity (see the calculation debate in Economics).

### What defenders say
- Many argue Stalinism betrayed Marx's aims, and point to anarchist and democratic-socialist traditions on the far left that opposed it.
- They credit revolutionary movements with ending feudal landholding, colonial rule and illiteracy in some countries, and with pushing capitalist states towards welfare reforms.

? Which far-left thinker warned that the Bolsheviks' methods would destroy democracy?
* Mao Zedong
+ Rosa Luxemburg
* Vladimir Lenin
= Luxemburg supported revolution but criticised Bolshevik suppression of free elections and a free press.

# Left wing · Social democracy and progressive liberalism

## Thinkers of the democratic left
The mainstream left accepts **democracy and a market economy**, but wants to make them fairer through the state: redistribution, public services, workers' rights and regulation.

- **Eduard Bernstein** (1850–1932), in _Evolutionary Socialism_ (1899), argued that capitalism was not collapsing as Marx had predicted, and that socialism should be pursued **gradually through elections and reforms**. This "revisionism" founded social democracy.
- **The Beveridge Report** (1942), by William Beveridge, proposed attacking the "five giants" of Want, Disease, Ignorance, Squalor and Idleness through social insurance and public services.
- **Anthony Crosland**, in _The Future of Socialism_ (1956), argued the goal was equality and social justice, not nationalising industry for its own sake.
- **John Rawls**, in _A Theory of Justice_ (1971), asked what rules people would choose behind a **"veil of ignorance"**, not knowing their own place in society. He argued they would choose equal basic liberties and allow inequalities only if they benefit the least advantaged (the **difference principle**).
- In 1959 Germany's Social Democrats (SPD) formally dropped Marxism at Bad Godesberg, accepting the market economy: "as much competition as possible, as much planning as necessary".

? What is Rawls's "veil of ignorance"?
* A rule that governments should keep policy secret
+ A thought experiment: choosing the rules of society without knowing your own position in it
* A theory that voters ignore politics
= Rawls argued that rules chosen impartially, without knowing whether you would be rich or poor, would be fair ones.

## The left in power and its critics
- **The United States**: Franklin D. Roosevelt's **New Deal** (from 1933) responded to the Great Depression with public works, Social Security, banking regulation and labour rights.
- **The United Kingdom**: Clement Attlee's Labour government (1945–51) created the **National Health Service** (1948), expanded social security, nationalised coal, rail and steel, and began decolonisation.
- **Sweden and the Nordic model**: long periods of Social Democratic government built universal welfare states, high taxes, strong unions and wage bargaining alongside open, competitive markets. Olof Palme (prime minister 1969–76 and 1982–86) was its best-known voice abroad.
- **West Germany**: Willy Brandt (chancellor 1969–74) combined social reform at home with _Ostpolitik_, reconciliation with Eastern Europe.

### What critics say
- **From the right**: high taxes and generous benefits can weaken incentives to work, save and invest; nationalised industries were often inefficient; the 1970s brought inflation, strikes and fiscal crises in several social democracies.
- **From the far left**: social democracy manages capitalism instead of replacing it.
- **Public choice theorists**: state programmes grow and serve the interests of those who run them.

### What defenders say
- The post-war decades of social democratic consensus combined rapid growth, full employment and falling inequality.
- The Nordic countries consistently rank among the most prosperous, equal and happy in the world.

? Which government created Britain's National Health Service?
* Winston Churchill's wartime coalition
+ Clement Attlee's Labour government
* Margaret Thatcher's Conservative government
= The NHS was launched in 1948 under Attlee, building on the Beveridge Report.

# Centre · Liberal centrism and the Third Way

## Thinkers of the centre
Centrists favour **pragmatism over ideology**: market economies with a social safety net, liberal democracy, the rule of law and gradual reform.

- **John Stuart Mill**, in _On Liberty_ (1859), defended individual freedom limited only by the **harm principle**: the only reason to restrict someone's liberty is to prevent harm to others.
- **Karl Popper**, in _The Open Society and Its Enemies_ (1945), attacked grand utopian blueprints (from Plato to Marx) and argued for **piecemeal social engineering**: small reforms that can be tested and corrected.
- **Christian democracy**, led after 1945 by Konrad Adenauer in Germany and Alcide De Gasperi in Italy, combined Christian social teaching with the **social market economy** (Ludwig Erhard): competitive markets plus social protection and worker consultation. It is often placed centre-right.
- **The Third Way**, named in Anthony Giddens's _The Third Way_ (1998), sought to renew the left by accepting globalisation and markets while investing in education and skills: "no rights without responsibilities".

? What is Mill's "harm principle"?
+ Liberty may only be restricted to prevent harm to others
* The state should prevent all harm, including to oneself
* Harmful ideas should be censored
= Mill argued that over things that concern only themselves, individuals should be sovereign.

## The centre in power and its critics
- **Bill Clinton** (US president 1993–2001) balanced the budget, signed welfare reform and free-trade agreements, and declared in 1996 that "the era of big government is over".
- **Tony Blair** (UK prime minister 1997–2007) rebranded Labour as "New Labour", rewrote Clause IV (dropping the commitment to public ownership) in 1995, gave the Bank of England independence, introduced a minimum wage and invested heavily in health and education. The 2003 Iraq war damaged his reputation.
- **Gerhard Schröder** (German chancellor 1998–2005) introduced the _Agenda 2010_ labour-market reforms.
- **Emmanuel Macron** founded _En Marche!_ in 2016 as "neither left nor right" and was elected French president in 2017 and re-elected in 2022.

### What critics say
- **From the left**: centrists accepted deregulation, financialisation and rising inequality, which helped cause the 2008 crisis and alienated working-class voters.
- **From the right**: centrists kept government large and taxes high while borrowing heavily.
- **Populists of both sides**: centrism represents a technocratic elite consensus that ignores voters' concerns on immigration, globalisation and identity.

### What defenders say
- Centrist governments delivered long periods of growth, falling crime and investment in public services, and made it possible to build broad coalitions in divided societies.

? Which party document did Tony Blair rewrite in 1995?
* The Beveridge Report
+ Labour's Clause IV on common ownership
* The Maastricht Treaty
= Clause IV committed Labour to common ownership of the means of production; Blair replaced it with a statement of democratic socialist values.

# Right wing · John Mearsheimer and the Realists

## The realist tradition
For this course the right is represented by **realism**, the school of international relations that sees world politics as a struggle for **power and security** among states. Realism is closer to the right than the left in its scepticism of idealism, its focus on national interest and its view of human nature, though realists themselves come from across party lines and often criticise right-wing as well as left-wing governments.

### Classical roots
- **Thucydides**, in his history of the Peloponnesian War (5th century BC), recorded the Athenian envoys telling the people of Melos that "the strong do what they can and the weak suffer what they must."
- **Niccolò Machiavelli**, in _The Prince_ (written 1513), argued rulers must be guided by what works, not by what ought to be.
- **Thomas Hobbes**, in _Leviathan_ (1651), described life without a sovereign as a "war of all against all". Between states there is no sovereign, so that condition persists internationally.

### Modern realism
- **E. H. Carr**, in _The Twenty Years' Crisis_ (1939), attacked the "utopian" liberal idealism of the inter-war years, which he blamed for failing to prepare for power politics.
- **Hans Morgenthau**, in _Politics Among Nations_ (1948), said states pursue **interest defined as power**, and that prudence, not universal moral rules, should guide foreign policy.
- **Kenneth Waltz**, in _Theory of International Politics_ (1979), founded **structural (neo-) realism**: the international system is **anarchic** (no world government), so all states must look after their own security, whatever their internal politics. He argued a bipolar world is more stable than a multipolar one.
- **Henry Kissinger** practised realism as US national security adviser and secretary of state (1969–77): détente with the Soviet Union and the opening to China.

? According to structural realists, why do states compete for power?
* Because all leaders are aggressive
+ Because there is no world government to protect them, so each must ensure its own security
* Because democracies always fight dictatorships
= Waltz located the cause in the anarchic structure of the international system, not in human nature or regime type.

## John Mearsheimer's offensive realism
John Mearsheimer (born 1947), a professor at the University of Chicago and a former US Air Force officer, is the leading advocate of **offensive realism**, set out in _The Tragedy of Great Power Politics_ (2001).

### The main ideas
1. The international system is anarchic.
2. Great powers have offensive military capabilities.
3. States can never be certain of others' intentions.
4. Survival is every state's primary goal.
5. States are rational actors.

From these assumptions Mearsheimer concludes that great powers seek to **maximise their relative power**, and the safest position is to become the **regional hegemon** (as the US is in the Western Hemisphere) while preventing any rival from dominating another region. This is the "tragedy": even states with no aggressive intent are pushed into security competition. Waltz's **defensive realists** (including Stephen Walt) disagree, arguing states seek only enough power to be secure.

### Positions and predictions
- **Europe after the Cold War** (1990): he predicted instability as the bipolar order ended, and argued against assuming peace was permanent.
- **China**: he argues China cannot rise peacefully; as it grows richer it will seek to dominate Asia, and the US will try to contain it, leading to intense security competition.
- **Ukraine**: in "Why the Ukraine Crisis Is the West's Fault" (_Foreign Affairs_, 2014) he argued that NATO and EU expansion towards Russia's border provoked Moscow, and that great powers react to rivals near their borders as the US did over Cuba in 1962. He has maintained this view since Russia's 2022 invasion.
- **Liberal hegemony**: in _The Great Delusion_ (2018) he argued that US attempts to spread democracy by force (for example, Iraq in 2003) fail and make the world less stable. He favours **offshore balancing**: intervening only to stop a rival hegemon.
- With Stephen Walt he wrote _The Israel Lobby and U.S. Foreign Policy_ (2007), arguing that lobbying pushes US policy against America's own interests. The book was strongly disputed.

### What critics say
- **Liberal institutionalists** (Robert Keohane, Joseph Nye) argue trade, institutions and democracy make cooperation possible and lasting; Europe's peace since 1945 is their evidence.
- **Constructivists** (Alexander Wendt: "anarchy is what states make of it", 1992) argue that ideas and identities, not structure alone, shape whether states see each other as enemies.
- On Ukraine, critics say Mearsheimer's view denies smaller states the right to choose their alliances, and underplays Russian imperial ambition as a cause of the war.
- Moral critics say realism treats ethics as irrelevant to foreign policy.

### What defenders say
- Realists warned early and accurately against the Iraq war, and argue their predictions about rising great-power rivalry with China and Russia have proved more accurate than liberal hopes after 1991.

> "The strong do what they can and the weak suffer what they must." — Thucydides, History of the Peloponnesian War (Melian Dialogue)

? What does Mearsheimer mean by the "tragedy" of great power politics?
* That great powers always lose wars
+ That even peaceful states are driven into security competition by the structure of the system
* That democracies cannot conduct foreign policy
= Because no state can be sure of others' intentions, all great powers are pushed to maximise power, even without aggressive aims.

? Which policy does Mearsheimer recommend for the United States?
+ Offshore balancing: intervening only to stop another region being dominated by a rival
* Spreading democracy worldwide through military intervention

# Far right · Fascism, national socialism and the radical right

## The ideas of the far right
The far right rejects the equality of all people and liberal democracy in favour of a **nation defined by ethnicity, culture or race**, led by a strong, often authoritarian state. This unit explains the ideas as historians and political scientists describe them, alongside their consequences.

### Fascism and Nazism
- **Benito Mussolini** came to power in Italy after the 1922 March on Rome. Italian fascism glorified the nation and the state above the individual, rejected both liberalism and socialism, used violence against opponents and created a one-party state. Mussolini's slogan was "Everything within the state, nothing outside the state, nothing against the state."
- **Adolf Hitler**'s **National Socialism** (Nazism) added a racial ideology: a belief in a superior "Aryan" race, virulent antisemitism and a demand for "living space" in the east. After 1933 the Nazi regime abolished democracy, persecuted Jews, political opponents, Roma, disabled people and others, and launched the Second World War. In the **Holocaust** it murdered about six million Jews.
- Other inter-war and wartime movements included Franco's nationalist dictatorship in Spain (1939–75) and collaborationist regimes in occupied Europe.

Historians such as Robert Paxton and Roger Griffin have described fascism's core as a myth of **national rebirth** after decline or humiliation, mass mobilisation, a cult of the leader, and the use of violence to purify the nation.

? According to historians such as Roger Griffin, what is a core myth of fascism?
* World peace through free trade
+ National rebirth after a period of decline or humiliation
* The abolition of all states
= Griffin described fascism as "palingenetic ultranationalism": the promise of a nation reborn.

## The far right after 1945
After 1945 fascism was discredited, but far-right parties and movements re-emerged. Political scientists distinguish:
- the **extreme right**, which rejects democracy itself and may endorse violence; and
- the **radical right**, which works within elections but opposes key liberal-democratic values such as minority rights and pluralism.

Cas Mudde (2007) identifies the **populist radical right** by three features:
1. **Nativism**: the state should be inhabited only by members of the native group; non-native people and ideas threaten the nation.
2. **Authoritarianism**: a strictly ordered society, with heavy emphasis on law and order.
3. **Populism**: society is divided between a "pure people" and a "corrupt elite".

France's Front National, founded in 1972 by Jean-Marie Le Pen, is a classic post-war example. Since the 2010s parties labelled radical right by many scholars have gained large vote shares in several democracies, usually campaigning on immigration, national sovereignty and opposition to established elites. Whether particular modern parties belong in this category is often contested by the parties themselves.

### What critics say
- Historical fascism and Nazism led to dictatorship, war and genocide, and their ideas of racial or ethnic hierarchy deny the equal dignity of people.
- Radical-right governments have been accused of weakening independent courts, the press and minority rights.

### What supporters of radical-right parties say
- They present themselves as defenders of national identity, sovereignty and ordinary voters' concerns about immigration and crime, which they say mainstream parties ignored.

! Note: Studying an ideology is not endorsing it. Understanding how the far right recruits support, and what happened when it held power, is part of understanding modern politics.

? Which three features does Cas Mudde use to define the populist radical right?
+ Nativism, authoritarianism and populism
* Socialism, internationalism and pacifism
* Free trade, low taxes and federalism
= Mudde's definition is widely used to compare these parties across countries.

## A crisis seen five ways
Imagine a large neighbouring country invades a smaller one that has been moving closer to a Western alliance. How would each area tend to respond?

- **Far left**: oppose the imperialism of both blocs; argue workers on both sides are victims of rival ruling classes; some groups have backed whichever side opposes Western power.
- **Left**: condemn the invasion as a breach of international law, support sanctions and humanitarian aid, work through the UN.
- **Centre**: support the victim through alliances, sanctions and military aid; strengthen collective security.
- **Realists (Mearsheimer)**: see the war as a predictable reaction by a great power to a rival alliance on its border; urge a negotiated settlement that recognises spheres of influence, and warn against escalation with a nuclear power.
- **Far right**: divided between nationalist hostility to the invader and admiration for a strong nationalist leader; often focused on keeping the home country out.

? Which area of the spectrum would most stress the balance of power and spheres of influence in this crisis?
* Left
* Centre
+ Right (Realists)
= Realists analyse such crises in terms of great-power security and relative power, not law or ideology.
