title: Economics
intro: Economics through its great thinkers and rival schools: Adam Smith and classical capitalism, Karl Marx and the socialists, Keynes and the Keynesians, Mises and the Austrian School, Milton Friedman and the Chicago School, and Richard Werner's credit creation theory. Each school is presented in its own terms, followed by its main criticisms.

# Introduction · How to study the schools

## Why economists disagree
Economists disagree for two kinds of reason.

- **Positive questions** are about how the economy works: does printing money cause inflation? Do minimum wages reduce employment? In principle, evidence can settle these, but data are noisy and experiments are rare.
- **Normative questions** are about what should be done: how much inequality is acceptable? How far should the state intervene? These depend on values as well as facts.

The schools in this course differ on both. They also start from different **methods**: Austrians reason from first principles about human action; Chicago economists test predictions statistically; Marx analysed history and class; Keynes focused on how real economies behave in a slump.

The same few questions divide them, and each unit returns to them:
1. Do markets tend to correct themselves, or can they stay stuck?
2. What causes booms and recessions?
3. What causes inflation?
4. Where does money come from, and who should control it?
5. What should the state do?

! How to use this course: Read each school as its supporters would explain it, then read its critics. The final unit puts all six side by side on the same questions.

? "The minimum wage should be raised because inequality is unjust." What kind of statement is this?
* A positive statement
+ A normative statement
* A statement of accounting fact
= It rests on a value judgement ("unjust"). A positive version would be "raising the minimum wage by 10% reduces teenage employment by X%", which evidence could test.

# Adam Smith · Classical economics and capitalism

## Adam Smith and The Wealth of Nations
Adam Smith (1723–1790) was a Scottish moral philosopher. His _Theory of Moral Sentiments_ (1759) studied sympathy and ethics; _An Inquiry into the Nature and Causes of the Wealth of Nations_ (1776) founded modern economics.

### The main ideas
- **Division of labour.** In Smith's pin factory, ten workers who each specialise in one step make thousands of times more pins than if each made whole pins alone. Specialisation, limited by the size of the market, is the main source of rising productivity.
- **Self-interest and exchange.** People trade because both sides gain.

> "It is not from the benevolence of the butcher, the brewer, or the baker, that we expect our dinner, but from their regard to their own interest." — Adam Smith, The Wealth of Nations (1776)

- **The invisible hand.** Individuals seeking their own gain in competitive markets are "led by an invisible hand" to promote the public good, which was no part of their intention. Prices coordinate millions of decisions without central direction.
- **Against mercantilism.** National wealth is not a stock of gold and silver but the goods and services people can consume. Tariffs and monopolies protect merchants at the public's expense, so Smith favoured free trade.
- **A limited but real role for government**: defence, justice and the rule of law, and public works and education that markets would not provide.

### What Smith is often misquoted on
Smith was suspicious of business power as well as state power:

> "People of the same trade seldom meet together, even for merriment and diversion, but the conversation ends in a conspiracy against the public, or in some contrivance to raise prices." — The Wealth of Nations

He argued for competition, not for whatever business wants, and he thought sympathy and moral norms were the foundation that markets rest on.

? In Smith's argument, what is the main source of a nation's wealth?
* Its stock of gold and silver
+ The productivity of its labour, raised by specialisation and trade
* Its trade surplus
= Smith rejected the mercantilist view that wealth is precious metal or trade surpluses. Wealth is what people can produce and consume, and specialisation multiplies it.

## Classical economics and modern capitalism
Smith's successors built the **classical school**:
- **David Ricardo** (1817) showed **comparative advantage**: even if England were worse than Portugal at making both cloth and wine, both countries gain if each specialises in what it is _relatively_ better at and trades. This remains the core argument for free trade.
- **Jean-Baptiste Say** argued that production creates the income to buy what is produced (**Say's law**), so a general glut of goods is impossible in the long run. Keynes later attacked this directly.
- **John Stuart Mill** (_Principles of Political Economy_, 1848) combined classical economics with liberal reforms.

### Capitalism in practice
"Capitalism" is an economy where the means of production are mostly privately owned and resources are allocated mainly through markets and prices. Its defenders point to:
- the unprecedented rise in living standards since the Industrial Revolution;
- innovation driven by profit and competition;
- economic freedom as a support for political freedom.

### Criticisms
- **Inequality**: market incomes can be very unequal (Marx's central charge).
- **Market failures**: pollution, monopoly and public goods are not handled well by markets alone.
- **Instability**: classical economics had little to say about mass unemployment, which Keynes made central.

? Portugal can make both wine and cloth more cheaply than England. According to Ricardo, should they trade?
+ Yes, each should specialise in what it is relatively best at
* No, Portugal should make everything itself
* Only if England uses tariffs
= Comparative advantage, not absolute advantage, determines the gains from trade. Both countries can consume more by specialising and trading.

# Karl Marx · Socialism and communism

## Marx's critique of capitalism
Karl Marx (1818–1883), a German philosopher, economist and revolutionary, wrote _The Communist Manifesto_ (1848, with Friedrich Engels) and _Capital_ (volume 1, 1867). He lived much of his life in London.

> "The history of all hitherto existing society is the history of class struggles." — Marx and Engels, The Communist Manifesto (1848)

### The main ideas
- **Historical materialism.** The way a society produces its material needs (its "mode of production") shapes its politics, law and ideas. Societies move through stages (feudalism, capitalism, then, Marx predicted, socialism) driven by conflict between classes.
- **Two classes.** Under capitalism the **bourgeoisie** own the means of production; the **proletariat** own only their ability to work and must sell it for wages.
- **Labour theory of value and surplus value.** Building on Smith and Ricardo, Marx argued that labour creates value. Workers produce more value in a day than they are paid in wages; the difference, **surplus value**, is taken as profit. That is what Marx meant by **exploitation**: not necessarily low pay, but the structural capture of surplus value.
- **Accumulation and crisis.** Competition forces capitalists to keep investing and replacing workers with machines. Marx argued this tends to lower the rate of profit, keeps a "reserve army" of unemployed workers, and produces recurring crises of overproduction.
- **Alienation.** Workers are cut off from the products of their work, from the work itself and from each other.
- **The future.** Marx expected workers to overthrow capitalism and, after a transitional stage, create a classless society organised on the principle:

> "From each according to his ability, to each according to his needs." — Marx, Critique of the Gotha Programme (1875)

? In Marx's theory, what is "surplus value"?
* The profit a shop makes from raising prices
+ The value workers produce beyond what they are paid in wages
* Savings held by workers in banks
= Marx argued that profit comes from the gap between the value labour creates and the wage paid for it.

## From Marx to socialist practice
Marx wrote little about how a socialist economy would actually work. His followers took very different routes.

- **Revolutionary communism.** Lenin led the 1917 Bolshevik revolution in Russia, arguing for a disciplined vanguard party. The Soviet Union replaced markets with **central planning**: the state set output targets and prices. Mao's China, Cuba and others followed versions of this model.
- **Social democracy.** Eduard Bernstein (1899) argued socialism could come gradually through elections. Social democratic parties built welfare states, public health systems and strong labour rights inside market economies, especially in post-war Western Europe and Scandinavia.
- **Market socialism.** Oskar Lange (1930s) proposed public ownership combined with prices that planners adjust like a market. Yugoslavia experimented with worker-managed firms.

### The socialist calculation debate
In 1920 Ludwig von Mises argued that without private ownership of capital there are **no market prices for capital goods**, so planners cannot calculate which uses of resources are efficient. Hayek added that the knowledge needed is dispersed among millions of people and cannot be gathered by a central planner. Lange replied that planners could find prices by trial and error.

### Criticisms and defences
- **Critics** point to chronic shortages and low productivity in planned economies, weak incentives, the absence of political freedom, and catastrophic famines under collectivisation in the Soviet Union and China. The labour theory of value is rejected by most modern economists in favour of marginal utility (see the Austrian unit).
- **Defenders** argue the Soviet model was a distortion of Marx, that Marx's analysis of inequality, crises and the power of capital remains relevant, and that social democratic reforms inspired by socialism (public pensions, health care, labour law) are now mainstream.

? What was Mises's "economic calculation problem"?
+ Without market prices for capital goods, planners cannot work out efficient uses of resources
* Computers in the 1920s were too slow for planning
* Socialist countries could not collect taxes
= Mises argued prices for capital goods only emerge from private ownership and exchange; without them, rational calculation of costs is impossible.

# Keynes · Keynes and the Keynesians

## Keynes and the General Theory
John Maynard Keynes (1883–1946) was a British economist, civil servant and investor. His _The General Theory of Employment, Interest and Money_ (1936), written during the Great Depression, changed economics and economic policy.

### The main ideas
- **Aggregate demand drives output in the short run.** Total spending (consumption, investment, government spending and net exports) determines how much is produced and how many people are employed.
- **Economies can get stuck.** Classical economists expected falling wages and interest rates to restore full employment. Keynes argued wages are sticky, falling wages also cut demand, and the economy can settle at an equilibrium with **involuntary unemployment**.
- **Investment and "animal spirits".** Business investment depends on uncertain expectations and confidence, not just interest rates, so it can collapse suddenly.
- **Liquidity preference.** People hold money for safety. In a slump the desire to hoard can be so strong that lower interest rates fail to revive spending (later called the **liquidity trap**).
- **The multiplier.** Extra spending becomes someone else's income, who spends part of it, and so on. An increase in government spending can raise total output by more than the initial amount.
- **The paradox of thrift.** If everyone tries to save more at once, spending falls, incomes fall, and total saving may not rise at all.

### Policy
In a slump the government should **borrow and spend** (or cut taxes) to replace missing private demand, and run surpluses in booms. Keynes also helped design the post-war **Bretton Woods** system of fixed exchange rates, the IMF and the World Bank (1944).

> "In the long run we are all dead." — Keynes, A Tract on Monetary Reform (1923), criticising economists who promise that markets will eventually correct themselves

? According to the paradox of thrift, what can happen if everyone tries to save more during a recession?
* Interest rates must rise
+ Spending and incomes fall, so total saving may not increase
* Inflation rises sharply
= One person's spending is another's income. If all cut spending together, incomes fall, which can leave total saving unchanged or lower.

## Keynesians after Keynes
Keynes's ideas were developed, simplified and contested.
- **The neoclassical synthesis** (John Hicks's IS–LM model, 1937; Paul Samuelson): Keynesian in the short run, classical in the long run. This dominated textbooks and policy from the 1940s to the 1970s.
- **The Phillips curve** (1958) suggested a stable trade-off: lower unemployment for somewhat higher inflation.
- **Stagflation** in the 1970s, with high inflation _and_ high unemployment, broke that trade-off and boosted Friedman's critique (next units).
- **New Keynesians** (from the 1980s) rebuilt the theory on micro-foundations: prices and wages are sticky because changing them is costly, so monetary policy has real short-run effects. This underpins modern central banking.
- **Post-Keynesians** (Joan Robinson, Hyman Minsky) stress fundamental uncertainty and finance. Minsky's **financial instability hypothesis** says stability breeds risk-taking and debt, which eventually produce a crash; many saw 2008 as a "Minsky moment".

### Criticisms
- **Crowding out**: government borrowing may raise interest rates and displace private investment.
- **Debt**: deficits are easier to start than to stop, and debt burdens future taxpayers.
- **Timing**: by the time spending takes effect, the recession may be over.
- **Public choice** (Buchanan): politicians have incentives to run deficits in good times as well as bad.
- **Austrians** argue stimulus prolongs malinvestment; **monetarists** argue monetary policy, not fiscal, is what matters.

? What did 1970s "stagflation" challenge?
* The idea that recessions exist
+ The idea of a stable trade-off between inflation and unemployment
* The existence of central banks
= High inflation and high unemployment together contradicted the simple Phillips curve that many Keynesian policies relied on.

# Mises · The Austrian School

## Mises, Menger and the Austrian method
The Austrian School began with **Carl Menger**, whose _Principles of Economics_ (1871) argued that value is **subjective**: a good is worth what people value it at the margin, not the labour put into it. (This "marginal revolution" happened at the same time in England and Switzerland.)

**Ludwig von Mises** (1881–1973) was its leading 20th-century figure. He wrote _The Theory of Money and Credit_ (1912), _Socialism_ (1922) and _Human Action_ (1949), and taught in Vienna before emigrating to the United States in 1940.

### The main ideas
- **Praxeology.** Economics is the logic of purposeful human action. Its core laws can be deduced from the fact that people act to achieve ends, so they do not need statistical testing (and cannot be overturned by it).
- **Methodological individualism.** Only individuals act; "the economy" is the result of their choices.
- **Subjective value and prices.** Prices are signals created by people's choices. They carry information no one person has.
- **Time preference and interest.** People prefer goods now to goods later; the interest rate reflects this and coordinates saving with investment (building on Eugen von Böhm-Bawerk).
- **The calculation problem.** Without private property and market prices, socialism cannot allocate resources rationally (see the Marx unit).
- **Sound money.** Mises defended the gold standard and opposed inflation, which he saw as a hidden tax that transfers wealth to those who receive new money first.

? According to the Austrian School, where does an object's economic value come from?
* The labour used to make it
+ The value individuals place on it at the margin
* The price the government sets
= Menger and the Austrians hold that value is subjective and marginal, in contrast to the labour theory of value used by Ricardo and Marx.

## Austrian business cycle theory and Hayek
Mises, and later **Friedrich Hayek** (Nobel Prize, 1974), developed the **Austrian business cycle theory (ABCT)**:
1. A central bank, or credit expansion by banks, pushes interest rates **below the natural rate** set by genuine saving.
2. Cheap credit makes long-term, capital-intensive projects look profitable: housing, factories, long supply chains.
3. But consumers have not actually chosen to save more, so resources are insufficient to finish all these projects. This is **malinvestment**.
4. When credit tightens or prices rise, the projects are revealed as unprofitable and the **bust** begins.
5. The recession is the necessary **liquidation** of malinvestment. Bailouts and stimulus only delay it and set up the next bubble.

> "There is no means of avoiding the final collapse of a boom brought about by credit expansion." — Mises, Human Action (1949)

Hayek also argued (_The Use of Knowledge in Society_, 1945) that the knowledge needed to run an economy is dispersed, local and often unspoken; market prices summarise it in a way no planner can. In _The Road to Serfdom_ (1944) he warned that central planning leads to political tyranny. Murray Rothbard later pushed the school towards anarcho-capitalism.

### Criticisms
- **Method**: rejecting empirical testing makes the theory hard to falsify.
- **Evidence**: studies find mixed support for ABCT; critics ask why entrepreneurs repeatedly fail to anticipate that low rates are temporary.
- **Policy**: "liquidationism" during the Great Depression is blamed by Keynesians and monetarists alike for deepening it.
- **Supporters** reply that the 2000s US housing bubble, fuelled by low rates and easy credit, fits the theory closely.

? In Austrian business cycle theory, what starts the boom?
* A rise in consumer confidence
+ Interest rates held below the rate set by genuine saving, through credit expansion
* An increase in government spending
= Artificially cheap credit sends false signals that encourage long-term projects which real saving cannot support.

# Friedman · The Chicago School

## Milton Friedman and monetarism
Milton Friedman (1912–2006) taught at the University of Chicago and won the Nobel Prize in 1976. He was the leading critic of post-war Keynesianism and a popular champion of free markets (_Capitalism and Freedom_, 1962; _Free to Choose_, 1980, with Rose Friedman).

### The main ideas
- **The quantity theory of money.** Starting from the equation of exchange:

$ M × V = P × Y      (money × velocity = price level × real output)

If velocity is fairly stable and output grows at its own pace, faster money growth mostly produces higher prices.

> "Inflation is always and everywhere a monetary phenomenon." — Milton Friedman

- **The Great Depression was a monetary failure.** In _A Monetary History of the United States, 1867–1960_ (1963, with Anna Schwartz), Friedman showed the US money stock fell by about a third between 1929 and 1933 as banks failed, and argued the Federal Reserve turned a recession into a depression by not stopping it.
- **The natural rate of unemployment.** In 1967–68 Friedman (and Edmund Phelps) argued there is no lasting trade-off between inflation and unemployment: once people expect higher inflation, unemployment returns to its natural rate and only inflation remains higher. Stagflation in the 1970s seemed to confirm this.
- **Rules, not discretion.** Because monetary policy works with "long and variable lags", central banks should follow a simple rule, such as growing the money supply at a steady few percent a year (the **k-percent rule**).
- **The permanent income hypothesis** (1957): people base spending on their expected long-run income, so temporary tax rebates have a smaller effect than Keynesians predicted.

### Policy beyond money
Friedman favoured floating exchange rates, a negative income tax instead of many welfare programmes, school vouchers and an all-volunteer army.

? According to monetarists, what is the main cause of sustained inflation?
* Strong trade unions
+ Money supply growing faster than real output
* High oil prices
= Supply shocks can raise prices once, but Friedman argued only persistent excess money growth can cause sustained inflation.

## The Chicago School beyond Friedman
The University of Chicago produced a broader tradition of market-based economics:
- **Frank Knight**: the difference between measurable risk and true uncertainty.
- **George Stigler**: **regulatory capture**, where regulators come to serve the industries they regulate.
- **Ronald Coase**: transaction costs, and the Coase theorem on resolving externalities through bargaining.
- **Gary Becker**: economics applied to crime, discrimination, family and **human capital**.
- **Robert Lucas**: **rational expectations** and the "Lucas critique": policies based on past relationships fail when people change their behaviour in response.
- **Eugene Fama**: the efficient market hypothesis (see the Finance course).

### Criticisms
- In the 1980s the link between money and inflation broke down as financial innovation made velocity unstable; central banks abandoned money-supply targets and moved to interest-rate targets.
- Critics argue rational-expectations models assume too much and missed the 2008 financial crisis.
- The "Chicago Boys" who advised Pinochet's Chile are controversial: supporters cite later growth, critics the dictatorship and the 1982 crash.
- Defenders note that inflation targeting, independent central banks and the focus on expectations are largely Friedman's legacy.

? What is "regulatory capture"?
* When the state nationalises an industry
+ When regulators end up serving the interests of the industry they regulate
* When a company buys a competitor
= Stigler argued that concentrated industries have strong incentives to influence their regulators, so regulation often benefits incumbents.

# Richard Werner · Credit creation and the Quantity Theory of Credit

## Where does money come from?
Richard Werner (born 1967) is a German economist who spent many years studying the Japanese economy and has held professorships at UK universities. He is known for three contributions: the **credit creation theory of banking**, the **Quantity Theory of Credit**, and coining the term **quantitative easing**.

### Three theories of banking
Werner distinguishes three views of what banks do:
1. **Financial intermediation**: banks collect savings and lend them on. They do not create money. (Common in modern textbooks before 2008.)
2. **Fractional reserve**: each bank lends out deposits, but the banking system as a whole multiplies central-bank reserves into a larger stock of money.
3. **Credit creation**: each individual bank creates new money when it lends, by crediting the borrower's account. No prior deposit or saving is needed.

In 2014 Werner published an empirical test: he observed a small German bank (Raiffeisenbank Wildenberg) as it made a loan and traced its accounting. The loan created a new deposit without any transfer of funds from elsewhere, which he argued supports the credit creation theory and rejects the other two. In the same year the Bank of England's _Money creation in the modern economy_ described lending as creating deposits.

! Why it matters: If banks create most money when they lend, then **who gets credit and for what** shapes the whole economy, and the central bank's control over money is indirect.

? In Werner's credit creation theory, what happens when a bank makes a loan?
* It transfers money from savers' accounts to the borrower
* It borrows reserves from the central bank first
+ It creates a new deposit for the borrower, adding new money
= Werner argues each bank creates money through lending; the deposit is created in the act of making the loan.

## The Quantity Theory of Credit, Japan and QE
Werner's **Quantity Theory of Credit** splits money (bank credit) by what it is used for:
- **Credit for the real economy**, meaning productive investment in goods and services that count in GDP, raises output and incomes, without inflation if it adds productive capacity.
- **Credit for consumption** raises demand without adding capacity, so it tends to cause **consumer-price inflation**.
- **Credit for financial and asset transactions** (property, shares, takeovers) does not enter GDP; it inflates **asset prices**, producing bubbles and later banking crises.

This, he argues, explains why the old quantity theory (MV = PY) seemed to break down: money spent on assets is counted in M but not in Y.

### Japan
In _Princes of the Yen_ (2001; English edition 2003), Werner argued that the Bank of Japan used informal **window guidance** (quotas on how much each bank could lend) to drive the 1980s property and stock bubble and the 1990s bust, which became Japan's "lost decade".

### Quantitative easing
In 1995, writing in Japan's _Nikkei_ newspaper, Werner proposed "quantitative easing": a policy to expand **credit creation for the real economy**, not simply lower interest rates. When central banks later adopted "QE" (Japan in 2001, the US and UK from 2008–09), they mostly bought bonds from financial institutions. Werner has criticised this version for boosting asset prices more than productive lending.

### Policy proposals
- Guide credit towards productive investment and away from speculation.
- Support many small, local, community-oriented banks, like Germany's savings and co-operative banks, which lend to small firms.
- Governments could fund themselves by borrowing from banks instead of issuing bonds, so that the borrowing creates new credit rather than recycling existing money.

### Criticisms
- Mainstream economists accept that lending creates deposits but argue banks are still constrained by capital rules, profitability, and the central bank's interest rate. They see Werner's framework as a useful emphasis rather than a revolution.
- Classifying credit by use is hard in practice: a loan to a firm can free up other money for speculation.
- Credit guidance risks steering credit to politically favoured sectors, a concern Austrian and Chicago economists raise about any directed lending.

? In the Quantity Theory of Credit, what does credit used to buy existing property or shares tend to cause?
* Higher GDP growth
* Consumer-price inflation
+ Rising asset prices and potential bubbles
= Werner argues asset-transaction credit does not enter GDP; it raises asset prices instead, which can end in a banking crisis.

# Comparing the schools

## Six answers to the same questions
| Question | Smith & classical | Marx & socialists | Keynes & Keynesians | Mises & Austrians | Friedman & Chicago | Werner |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Do markets self-correct? | Yes, broadly, through competition | No; crises are built into capitalism | Not reliably; demand can stay too low | Yes, if left alone | Yes, if money is stable | Depends on where credit goes |
| Main cause of recessions | Rarely analysed (Say's law) | Overproduction, falling profit rate, class conflict | Collapse in aggregate demand | Bust after credit-fuelled malinvestment | Monetary contraction or policy mistakes | Credit shrinking or flowing into asset bubbles |
| Cause of inflation | Excess money (early quantity theory) | Secondary concern; conflict over income shares | Excess demand, cost pressures | Credit and money expansion | Excess money growth | Credit for consumption |
| Who creates money? | Mints and banks; gold-based | — | Central bank and banks; endogenous in post-Keynesian view | Banks via credit expansion; should be backed by gold | Central bank controls money supply | Commercial banks, when they lend |
| Role of the state | Defence, justice, public works | Own or direct the means of production (or regulate heavily) | Manage demand; stabilise the cycle | Minimal; protect property and sound money | Limited; stable monetary rule, light regulation | Guide credit; support local banks |
| Policy in a slump | Wait; remove barriers to trade | Structural change | Fiscal stimulus, low rates | Let malinvestment liquidate | Stop money supply falling | Expand credit for productive investment |

! Remember: Real economists often mix these views. Most modern central banks combine New Keynesian models with Friedman's focus on expectations and inflation targets, and since 2008 they pay more attention to credit and banking, as Minsky and Werner urged.

## A recession seen six ways
Imagine a country after a property boom. House prices fall 25%, banks have bad loans, unemployment is rising and inflation is low. How would each school read it?

- **Adam Smith and the classical school**: wages and prices will adjust; keep markets open and the state from protecting failing businesses.
- **Marx**: a typical capitalist crisis; workers lose jobs while the losses of capital are socialised; the system's contradictions are showing.
- **Keynes**: demand has collapsed; the government should borrow and spend on investment until private demand recovers, and not cut spending.
- **Mises and the Austrians**: the boom was caused by cheap credit; the bust is the cure. Let bad banks fail, allow prices to fall, and avoid bailouts that plant the next bubble.
- **Friedman**: above all, the central bank must stop the money supply shrinking as banks fail. Provide liquidity; don't repeat 1929–33.
- **Werner**: the boom came from credit for property speculation; recovery needs new credit for productive businesses, through central-bank action aimed at the real economy and a healthier local banking sector.

? Which school would most strongly oppose bailing out banks after the crash?
* Keynesian
* Monetarist (Chicago)
+ Austrian
= Austrians see bailouts as preventing the necessary liquidation of malinvestment and creating moral hazard. Friedman's followers, by contrast, focus on stopping the money supply from collapsing.

? Which thinker would focus most on what the new credit is used for?
* Adam Smith
* Milton Friedman
+ Richard Werner
= Werner's Quantity Theory of Credit separates credit by use: productive investment, consumption or asset purchases.
