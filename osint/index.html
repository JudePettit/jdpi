Archive well

  • Preserve first, analyse second. Save an independent copy (the Wayback Machine) and your own copy (SingleFile or ArchiveWeb.page, plus a full-page screenshot), then hash your files.
  • Check the capture, not just the “saved” message: pages built by scripts or behind logins often capture badly.
  • An archive proves what a page said at a moment, not that it was true, and it is not a way round a paywall or login.

More in the Kit Guide and the course lesson Archives and preservation.

Sanctions and watchlists (OpenSanctions)

Needs your key

OpenSanctions combines sanctions lists, watchlists and politically exposed persons from hundreds of sources. Anyone can search its website (opens in a new tab) without a key. The API needs one: free for public-interest work (journalists, non-profits working against corruption and financial crime, academic researchers; OpenSanctions checks before granting it), while anyone else gets 2,000 trial credits on sign-up and then pays per query. Hobby projects and business screening don’t qualify for a free key. Who qualifies (opens in a new tab).

Your key stays in this browser (only if you tick “Remember”) and is sent only to api.opensanctions.org, in the request address: the API’s browser check refuses the usual Authorization header. Screening here covers organisations, vessels and aircraft, not people.

    What metadata can and can’t prove

    Metadata is what the camera, phone or software wrote into the file: the device, the time, sometimes a GPS position and an editing history.

    • Absence proves nothing. Social networks and messaging apps strip metadata on upload, so most images found online have none.
    • Presence proves little. Anyone can edit these fields with free tools. Camera clocks are often wrong or on another time zone, and GPS fixes can be tens of metres out.
    • It is a lead, not a verdict. Confirm a time with shadows and weather, a place with satellite and street-level imagery, and the image’s history with reverse image search.
    • Content Credentials (C2PA), where present, record a signed history; they show who signed it, not that the scene is real.

    Course: Reverse image search, metadata and synthetic media.

    Reverse image search

    You open each engine yourself

    Search engines can’t receive a file from this page. For a file on your computer, open an engine and drop the file into it. For an image already online, paste its address and the links search it directly. Crop to distinctive details and try a mirrored copy too.

    Use reverse image search to trace images of places and events, never to identify private people from their faces.

    Sources and services. Live lookups go directly from your browser to the services named on each tab: the Internet Archive’s Wayback Machine (availability API; Save Page Now opens on archive.org), the RDAP servers of the domain registries and regional internet registries, found through IANA’s RDAP bootstrap files (with rdap.org as a fallback), Google Public DNS and Cloudflare’s DNS-over-HTTPS JSON APIs, crt.sh and SSLMate’s Cert Spotter (certificate transparency), the GLEIF API (LEI data under CC0), OpenSanctions (with your key; data under CC BY-NC 4.0) and the GDELT Project’s DOC 2.0 API. Map tiles: Esri. Everything else opens as a link on the service’s own site.

    Privacy. PRP receives nothing you type here. The case notebook, your settings, any API key you choose to remember and copies of your last few GDELT results are kept in this browser’s local storage; clearing site data deletes them, so export your notebook. Images you check are read on your device and never uploaded.

    Method. Search links are built from your fields in each engine’s own syntax; where an engine lacks an operator, its card says what it ignores. Archive checks use the Wayback availability API, which returns the capture closest to a date; the full capture list (CDX) cannot be read by browser pages, so the page opens it for you to paste back. GDELT requests are spaced at least six seconds apart, and when GDELT is busy the Events tab shows the last copy of the same query saved in this browser, marked with the time it was saved.
